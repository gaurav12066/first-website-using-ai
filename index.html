An illustrated guide

The ocean gets stranger the deeper you go.

Sunlight disappears within the first 200 metres. Below that, over 90% of the ocean's volume sits in permanent darkness, freezing cold, and crushing pressure — and it's still mostly unmapped. Here's what's actually down there, zone by zone.

The five depth zones

Oceanographers divide the water column into zones by how much light reaches them and how conditions change with depth.

0–200 mSunlight zone

Epipelagic

Enough light for photosynthesis, so nearly all ocean life ultimately depends on this thin surface layer. Home to tuna, dolphins, and coral reefs.

200–1,000 mTwilight zone

Mesopelagic

Light fades to nothing by the bottom of this zone. Many animals here migrate upward each night to feed, then sink back down by day.

1,000–4,000 mMidnight zone

Bathypelagic

No sunlight at all. Water temperature holds near 4°C year-round, and pressure would crush an unprotected human instantly.

4,000–6,000 mAbyssal zone

Abyssopelagic

Covers over half the ocean floor. Near-freezing, pitch black, and under pressure exceeding 400 times that at the surface.

6,000 m+Trenches

Hadopelagic

Found only in ocean trenches. The Mariana Trench's Challenger Deep reaches nearly 11,000 metres — deeper than Everest is tall.

Who lives down there

A few residents of the deep, adapted to darkness, cold, and pressure most surface life could never survive.

Anglerfish

Females dangle a glowing lure grown from a modified fin spine to draw prey close in the pitch-black midnight zone.

Giant squid

Eyes up to 27 cm across — the largest of any animal — help it catch faint traces of light and movement in deep water.

Giant tube worm

Has no mouth or gut. It lives off bacteria in its tissue that convert chemicals from hydrothermal vents into food.

Dumbo octopus

Lives deeper than any other octopus, gliding above the seafloor using ear-like fins rather than jet propulsion.

A short glossary

Pelagic
Relating to the open water column, as opposed to the seafloor or shoreline.
Bioluminescence
Light produced by a living organism through a chemical reaction inside its body.
Hydrothermal vent
A fissure on the seafloor that releases geothermally heated, mineral-rich water.
Marine snow
A constant drift of organic debris falling from upper waters, a key food source in the deep sea.
Diel vertical migration
The daily movement of marine animals upward at night to feed and downward by day to hide.
